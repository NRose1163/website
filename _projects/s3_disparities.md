This poster was presented at the S3 Student Symposum in 2025 and was awarded the Provost's Award for best undergraduate poster from the College of Arts and Letters. It displays my work studying the disparities in self-reported disability rates, Supplemental Security Income (SSI), Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) and Medicaid participation rates between same-sex and different-sex coupled individuals. It's an offshoot of a policy brief I authored for the Social Insurance Research Fellowship in summer 2024 with the [Center for Health Economics and Policy Studies](https://cheps.sdsu.edu/). 


This descriptive study uses data from the American Community Survey (ACS) from 2017-2022 aims to understand baseline differences between LGBTQ+ and heterosexual indivuals in terms of disability and assistance take up rates and how COVID-19 shocks may have affected each group differntly. Because there are no questions regarding sexual orientation or gender identity (SOGI) in the ACS, cohabitating couples were matched by gender to indicate whether respondent are in a same-sex or different-sex relationship. Weighted averages of demographic, socioeconomic, education and health indicators over the period are used to describe each group, as well as timeseries figures used to understand how self-reported disability rates, SSDI, SSI and Medicaid particpation rates changed over time. 


Analysis shows that same-sex couples are about five years younger than different-sex couples and less likely to be married (60% vs. 88%). They have about one-third as many children in the household, and they have higher employment (75% vs. 66%), median household income (\$105,000 vs. \$95,400), and educational attainment (49% vs. 39% with a bachelor’s degree or higher). They also report higher rates of disability and higher participation in SSDI, SSI, and Medicaid. From 2017 to 2023, both groups show the same general trends: SSDI and SSI participation fall while Medicaid participation rises. After the COVID-19 shocks, the gaps between the groups narrow. Same-sex couples see a larger drop in Social Security program participation, and their Medicaid participation peaks around 2022 and begins to decline by 2023. Together, these patterns point to a possible shift from Social Security programs toward Medicaid. The results are descriptive and lay the groundwork for future causal study.


This study excludes single individuals who may identify as LGBTQ+, because same-sex coupled individuals can only be identified among cohabiting households. Understanding disparities between LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ populations is essential for designing effective policy aimed at reducing inequities. A major barrier to this research is that many nationally representative datasets, including the ACS, do not ask about sexual orientation or gender identity. Complete SOGI identification in nationally representative datasets like the ACS would make it possible to include single individuals and and better study differences between people who identify as LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ
